How games make MONEY

How games make MONEY

Did you know? In 2025, people spent more money on mobile games than on PC and console games combined.

Who gets your money:

  1. Platform holder: Steam, PlayStation, Xbox, Nintendo, Apple, and Google usually take about 30% of every sale or in-app purchase. Some offer lower rates on bigger sales or for smaller developers.

  2. Publisher: The publisher funds development and marketing, so it often keeps a large share until it has recouped those costs. After that, revenue is split with the studio according to their contract.

  3. Developer: The studio that actually built the game gets a cut from the publisher, or most of what remains if it self-published. This pays salaries, tools, and the next project.

  4. Taxes and other costs: VAT or sales tax (19% in Germany) is included in the price you pay, plus payment processing fees, engine licenses like Unreal or Unity, and music or middleware licensing.

Pro tips

  1. Wait for sales and buy late: Most games drop 30 to 70% within a year, and Steam, PlayStation, and Xbox run big seasonal sales (Black Friday is just around the corner) Useful link. Keep a wishlist so you get notified of discounts, and let your backlog shrink before buying anything new. Subscriptions like Game Pass or PS Plus can also be cheaper than buying if you mostly play new releases once and move on.

  2. Set a monthly budget and watch out for microtransactions: Decide on a fixed amount per month, and treat in-game purchases (skins, battle passes, loot boxes) as part of it. Small purchases add up quickly, so consider removing saved payment details or using prepaid gift cards to create a natural spending limit.

Revenue:
Gaming revenue is the lifeblood of the industry, and it flows through more channels than most players realize. Premium game sales still form the foundation, with a single hit title capable of earning hundreds of MILLIONS of dollars within days of launch. On top of that sit digital add-ons like DLC, expansions, and season passes, which stretch a game’s earning life for years after release. Live-service games turn a one-time sale into a steady income stream through battle passes, cosmetic shops, and in-game currencies, while subscriptions such as Game Pass and PS Plus bring predictable monthly revenue. Mobile gaming dominates the volume, generating roughly half of the global market through ads and in-app purchases. Add esports sponsorships, streaming partnerships, merchandise, and licensing deals, and a single franchise can become a multi-channel money machine. Studios track key numbers like ARPU (average revenue per user), conversion rate, and lifetime value to squeeze the maximum from each player, because in modern gaming, revenue is not just earned at launch, it is engineered over time.

The Dark Side of Revenue:
Behind the glossy numbers lies a darker playbook designed to keep your wallet open. Loot boxes borrow mechanics from gambling, wrapping random rewards in flashy animations that trigger the same reward loops as slot machines, which is why countries like Belgium and the Netherlands have restricted them. Virtual currencies such as gems and coins disguise real prices, making it harder to feel how much you are actually spending. Limited-time offers and daily login rewards create artificial pressure, pushing players to buy now out of fear of missing out. Many free-to-play titles depend on a tiny group of “whales” for the bulk of their income, and some of those players are vulnerable people who spend far beyond their means. Pay-to-win designs, aggressive paywalls, and difficulty spikes tuned to push you toward a purchase can turn a game into a chore unless you pay. Children are especially at risk, with unauthorized purchases leading to refund battles and angry parents. When profit becomes the main design goal, the line between entertainment and exploitation gets dangerously thin.
